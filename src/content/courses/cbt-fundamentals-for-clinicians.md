## Why CBT, and why this refresher

Cognitive-behavioral therapy remains one of the most extensively researched therapeutic modalities, with strong evidence across anxiety disorders, depression, and a range of other presentations. This short course is a practical refresher on the core mechanics clinicians use session to session — not a full training, but a grounding in the pieces that make CBT work.

## The cognitive model

The central idea in CBT is straightforward: it is rarely the situation alone that determines how someone feels, but the thoughts and interpretations that arise in response to it. Two clients can face the same stressor — a delayed reply to a text message, for instance — and land in very different emotional places depending on the automatic thought that surfaces ("they're upset with me" versus "they're probably just busy").

This doesn't mean thoughts are the *only* thing that matters — behavior and physiology feed back into the loop too — but it's the lever CBT is built around.

## Automatic thoughts and cognitive distortions

Automatic thoughts are the fast, often unexamined interpretations that pop up in response to a situation. Part of the clinician's job is helping a client notice these thoughts rather than treating them as fact.

Common distortions worth recognizing and naming with clients include:

- **Catastrophizing** — assuming the worst-case outcome is not just possible but likely.
- **All-or-nothing thinking** — viewing situations in absolute, binary terms.
- **Mind reading** — assuming you know what others are thinking, usually negatively, without evidence.
- **Overgeneralization** — drawing a broad, sweeping conclusion from a single event.

## The thought record

A thought record is one of the most practical tools in CBT. In its simplest form, it walks a client through:

1. The situation
2. The automatic thought
3. The emotion and its intensity
4. Evidence for the thought
5. Evidence against the thought
6. A more balanced, alternative thought

The goal isn't to force positivity — it's to test the automatic thought against actual evidence, the same way you'd examine any other hypothesis.

## Structuring a session

A typical CBT session tends to follow a consistent shape, which itself is part of what makes the approach effective — clients know what to expect, and sessions stay focused:

1. Brief mood check-in
2. Review of homework from the previous session
3. Collaborative agenda-setting for today's session
4. Work on the agenda items (skills, thought records, exposure planning, etc.)
5. Assigning new homework
6. Summary and feedback

## A closing note

This refresher only scratches the surface — behavioral activation, exposure hierarchies, and case conceptualization each deserve their own deep dive. But the cognitive model, awareness of distortions, the thought record, and consistent session structure form the backbone that most CBT work builds on.
